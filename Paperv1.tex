\section{Introduction}
Saliency maps are the dominant explanation in medical imaging AI: most of 
221 surveyed medical XAI papers explain a prediction by highlighting the 
image regions that drove it~\cite{wagner2026usercentric}. Yet their value is 
disputed: \citet{hatherley2026defence} defend post hoc explanation, while 
\citet{kaufman2023radiology} argue it does not match how experts build a 
diagnosis. The dispute is hard to settle in general because explanation 
quality depends on the model and task: Grad-CAM's fidelity holds for CNNs 
but drops for Vision Transformers~\cite{panboonyuen2026seeing}, and method 
rankings change with metric and dataset~\cite{ma2026ranking}.

A method that works well for one model therefore cannot be assumed to work 
for another, and must be checked on the model it explains. A paper that 
presents a map as an explanation implies that the map reflects what its 
model computes, a property called \emph{faithfulness}. This differs from 
\emph{plausibility}, whether the map matches where a clinician would 
look~\cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}, and the two must be tested separately 
because a map can pass one and fail the other. In our experiment, the method 
that located the tumour best depended least on the model 
(Sec.~\ref{sec:experiment}).

Only one of the six recent papers we audit reports a working faithfulness 
test. \citet{panboonyuen2026seeing} defines faithfulness metrics but reports 
none and concludes from ten example images. The others present maps that 
look right as evidence that their models reason correctly, without showing 
that the maps reflect the models at all.

To close this gap, we contribute (1) a framework separating faithfulness, 
plausibility and simulatability; (2) an audit of six papers against it; 
(3) an experiment showing what the missing tests reveal; and (4) a minimum 
reporting checklist.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
 
\subsection{Saliency Methods}
Saliency methods score each pixel by its contribution to a prediction. Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} weights each feature map $A^k$ of the last convolutional layer by the averaged gradient of the class score $y^c$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_k^c = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_i\sum_j \frac{\partial y^c}{\partial A^k_{ij}}, \qquad
L^c = \mathrm{ReLU}\Big(\sum_k \alpha_k^c A^k\Big),
\end{equation}
where $Z$ is the number of spatial positions $(i,j)$, so its maps are coarse and can blur small lesions. IG~\cite{sundararajan2017ig} integrates gradients from a baseline image to the input, LRP~\cite{bach2015lrp} propagates the output score backwards while conserving relevance, and SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap} assigns Shapley values, usually computed over superpixels for images. All four are \emph{post-hoc}: they explain an already-trained model, whereas self-explainable models build interpretability into the architecture~\cite{hou2024sxai}, which \citet{rudin2019stop} argues high-stakes domains should prefer.
 
\subsection{Faithfulness, Plausibility and Simulatability}
\label{sec:properties}
An explanation is \emph{faithful} if it reflects the reasoning the model used, and \emph{plausible} if it convinces a human~\cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}; in medical imaging, plausibility is usually judged by overlap with lesion masks. A map can outline a tumour while the model relies on something else, so faithfulness must be tested against the model. The \emph{randomisation test}~\cite{adebayo2018sanity} randomises the model's weights: a map that barely changes cannot reflect what the model learned. The \emph{deletion test}~\cite{petsiuk2018rise} removes the most salient pixels first: for a faithful map, the class score falls faster than when random pixels are removed. \emph{Simulatability}, whether a person can predict the model's output from the explanation, is a third property, which CX-ToM~\cite{akula2022cxtom} measures as users' ability to predict the model. \citet{hou2024sxai} list faithfulness and plausibility as separate requirements for medical explanations.
 
\subsection{Known Failures}
Under the randomisation test, Guided Backpropagation and Guided Grad-CAM maps barely changed, while Grad-CAM passed~\cite{adebayo2018sanity}. On chest radiographs, all eight methods tested by \citet{arun2021trustworthiness} failed at least one trustworthiness criterion, and all seven tested by \citet{saporta2022benchmarking} localised pathologies worse than radiologists. \citet{degrave2021shortcuts} showed why this matters clinically: COVID-19 classifiers relied on shortcuts such as text markers rather than lung pathology.
\section{Audit Methodology}
\label{sec:audit-framework}
We examine whether four selected papers support their explanation claims, keeping faithfulness, plausibility and simulatability (Section~\ref{sec:properties}) separate from user ratings and diagnosis decisions. For each paper, we compare the claim, test and reported outcome, distinguishing visual examples, proposed measures, reported tests and unclear scores. Missing evidence does not mean a method fails. We selected these cases for their different forms of evidence, not to estimate reporting rates across the field.

\section{Audit Results}
\label{sec:audit-results}
\textbf{Proposed measures.} \citet{panboonyuen2026seeing} defines localisation, perturbation faithfulness and consistency measures in Section II-C. However, Table I reports classification performance, while the explanation analysis discusses example heatmaps. We found no numerical outcomes for the three explanation measures. The examples suggest differences worth testing, but do not establish which architecture produces more faithful maps.

\textbf{Combined explanations.} \citet{mcgonagle2026combined} presents Grad-CAM, LRP and SHAP together for brain tumour detection. Sections 4.1 and 4.2 show example maps, masks and percentages of positive and negative SHAP values. These describe the explanations, but do not independently validate them. We found no randomisation test, controlled deletion test, aggregate mask-overlap score or user evaluation. Agreement between maps therefore does not establish that they explain what the model used.

\textbf{Reported fidelity.} \citet{wen2025transparent} reports fidelity scores for Grad-CAM, IG and SHAP across five medical image datasets. Section VI-C describes fidelity as a comparison of original and perturbed prediction confidence, with results in Figure 16. The paper does not make clear how the map selects the perturbation or specify the attack and its strength. The score may describe classifier robustness rather than explanation faithfulness.

\textbf{User evaluation.} \citet{wagner2026usercentric} compares eight formats and no explanation with 33 medical participants, including medical students. Bounding box plus report received the highest mean ratings across the four assessed areas; heatmaps scored below no explanation on understandability. These findings concern user responses, not model faithfulness or simulatability. One correct and one incorrect diagnosis per format also limit generalisation.

Across these cases, visual agreement, attribution values and user preference answer different questions. None alone establishes whether a map reflects the model. Our experiment in Section~\ref{sec:experiment} examines this distinction using several checks on the same maps.

\section{Experiment}
\label{sec:experiment}
This section shows what the missing tests reveal on a typical medical imaging setup. We train a standard classifier, explain it with four common saliency methods, and apply one plausibility test and two faithfulness tests to the same maps. Code and results are in the project repository.

\subsection{Setup}
\textbf{Data.} The LGG MRI Segmentation dataset~\cite{buda2019lgg} contains 3,929 axial brain MRI slices from 110 patients with lower-grade glioma, each with a manual tumour mask. The pre-contrast, FLAIR and post-contrast sequences are stacked as the three channels of a $256\times256$ image, and a slice is labelled \emph{tumour} if its mask contains any tumour pixel. Patients are split 77/16/17 into training, validation and test sets, so no patient appears in more than one set.

\textbf{Model.} An ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18~\cite{he2016resnet} is fine-tuned on the training patients and reaches, on the 674 test slices, accuracy 0.942, AUC 0.976, sensitivity 0.837 and specificity 0.987.

\textbf{Explanations.} We compare Gradient~\cite{simonyan2014saliency}, Guided Backpropagation~\cite{springenberg2015guided}, Integrated Gradients (IG; 32 steps, black baseline)~\cite{sundararajan2017ig} and Grad-CAM on the last convolutional block~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}. All four explain the tumour logit on the 169 test tumour slices the model classifies correctly.

\subsection{Randomisation Test}
Following \citet{adebayo2018sanity}, we re-initialise the network one block at a time, from the output layer down to the first convolution, with three random seeds. After each step we compare the new maps with the trained model's maps using Spearman rank correlation of absolute saliency over brain pixels. A faithful method should lose its similarity as the learned weights are destroyed. Table~\ref{tab:results} gives the results.

Gradient and Grad-CAM pass: their similarity drops sharply once the output layer is randomised and is near zero for the fully random network. Guided Backpropagation fails in the way \citet{adebayo2018sanity} reported, staying almost unchanged (0.99) when the output layer is randomised, so its map does not depend on the class the model learned. IG fails differently, keeping a correlation of 0.54 with a \emph{completely random} network. Its maps correlate with raw input intensity at 0.66 for the trained model and 0.67 for the random model, so a large share of each IG map reflects the image rather than the model.

Over the whole image, IG's similarity with the fully random network is 0.82 rather than 0.54, because with a black baseline IG is exactly zero on the black background whatever the weights.

\subsection{Localisation}
We measure plausibility as it is usually reported, by agreement with the annotated tumour: the pointing game~\cite{zhang2018pointing} (whether the map's peak falls inside the mask) and the share of the map's mass inside the mask. The tumour covers on average 2.8\% of a slice, so a uniform map would place 2.8\% of its mass there. As controls we score the fully randomised model and two maps that use no model at all: Sobel edges of the FLAIR channel and raw input intensity.

Table~\ref{tab:results} shows that IG localises the tumour best, with its peak inside the mask on 91\% of slices. Yet IG is also the method that keeps the most similarity with a fully random network in the same table.

\subsection{Deletion Test}
Following \citet{petsiuk2018rise}, we black out the top 1--20\% of the image ranked by saliency and measure the drop in the model's tumour probability, whose baseline is 0.967. We rank either single pixels or $16\times16$ patches (by mean saliency). As controls we delete the same amount of randomly chosen brain pixels or patches, and the annotated tumour itself.

At pixel level the test cannot tell a saliency method from chance (Table~\ref{tab:results}): deleting 1\% of random brain pixels lowers the tumour probability by 0.83, as much as deleting the pixels Gradient, Guided Backpropagation or IG rank highest, and slightly more than deleting the whole tumour. Scattered black pixels push the image out of the distribution the model was trained on and break its prediction whatever selects them~\cite{hooker2019roar}. Grad-CAM, whose coarse maps rank contiguous regions, behaves like the patch version even at pixel level. Deleting contiguous patches removes this effect: random patches lower the probability by only 0.04, while every method's top patches lower it by 0.37--0.49. Under this version of the test, all four methods highlight regions the model relies on.

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recommendations}
The experiment shows why the evidence missing from the audited papers matters. First, plausibility does not imply faithfulness: IG localised the tumour best yet failed randomisation, so localisation alone, the evidence most papers report, would rank the methods wrongly, consistent with \citet{ma2026ranking}. Second, no single test is enough: Guided Backpropagation and IG passed patch deletion while failing randomisation. Third, how a test is run decides its outcome: including the background raised IG's similarity to a random network from 0.54 to 0.82, and pixel-level deletion made every method indistinguishable from random. A paper presenting saliency maps as explanations should therefore report, for the model it explains:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a randomisation test over the region of interest, not the whole image;
\item a deletion test on contiguous regions, with the replacement value stated and a random-region control;
\item localisation against lesion masks, alongside no-model baselines such as edge maps;
\item aggregate scores over a stated test set, not selected example maps;
\item user studies that include incorrect model outputs and measure whether users detect them~\cite{wagner2026usercentric}.
\end{enumerate}
Items 1--4 need only the trained model and its test data.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Saliency maps in medical imaging are presented as evidence that a model reasons correctly, but the evidence offered is mostly plausibility: example maps that look right and formats users prefer. None of the four audited papers reports a result showing that its maps reflect the model, and our experiment shows that such results can reverse which method looks trustworthy.

\textbf{Limitations.} The audited papers were chosen by hand and assessed by the authors, so the audit shows a pattern, not how common it is. The experiment uses one dataset, architecture and training run, with 169 slices from 17 patients. Black replacement in deletion~\cite{hooker2019roar}, batch-norm resetting in randomisation and IG's single black baseline may each affect the size of the effects. Simulatability was not measured, and how to measure faithfulness when the model's reasoning cannot be observed remains open.

Future work should repeat the experiment across architectures, datasets and replacement schemes, and test with clinicians whether faithful maps help them catch model errors. Until the checks in Section~\ref{sec:recommendations} are standard, a saliency map in a medical imaging paper should be read as a plausible illustration, not as evidence of how the model decides.
