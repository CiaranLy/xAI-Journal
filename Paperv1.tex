\section{Introduction}
Saliency maps are the dominant explanation in medical imaging AI: most of 
221 surveyed medical XAI papers explain a prediction by highlighting the 
image regions that drove it~\cite{wagner2026usercentric}. Yet their value is 
disputed: \citet{hatherley2026defence} defend post hoc explanation, while 
\citet{kaufman2023radiology} argue it does not match how experts build a 
diagnosis. The dispute is hard to settle in general because explanation 
quality depends on the model and task: Grad-CAM's fidelity holds for CNNs 
but drops for Vision Transformers~\cite{panboonyuen2026seeing}, and method 
rankings change with metric and dataset~\cite{ma2026ranking}.

A method that works well for one model therefore cannot be assumed to work 
for another, and must be checked on the model it explains. A paper that 
presents a map as an explanation implies that the map reflects what its 
model computes, a property called \emph{faithfulness}. This differs from 
\emph{plausibility}, whether the map matches where a clinician would 
look~\cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}, and the two must be tested separately 
because a map can pass one and fail the other. In our experiment, the method 
that located the tumour best depended least on the model 
(Sec.~\ref{sec:experiment}).

Only one of the five recent papers we audit reports a working faithfulness 
test. \citet{panboonyuen2026seeing} defines faithfulness metrics but reports 
none and concludes from ten example images. The others present maps that 
look right as evidence that their models reason correctly, without showing 
that the maps reflect the models at all.

To close this gap, we contribute (1) a framework separating faithfulness, 
plausibility and simulatability; (2) an audit of five papers against it; 
(3) an experiment showing what the missing tests reveal; and (4) a minimum 
reporting checklist.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
 
\subsection{Saliency Methods}
Saliency methods score each pixel by its contribution to a prediction. Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} weights each feature map $A^k$ of the last convolutional layer by the averaged gradient of the class score $y^c$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_k^c = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_i\sum_j \frac{\partial y^c}{\partial A^k_{ij}}, \qquad
L^c = \mathrm{ReLU}\Big(\sum_k \alpha_k^c A^k\Big),
\end{equation}
where $Z$ is the number of spatial positions $(i,j)$, so its maps are coarse and can blur small lesions. IG~\cite{sundararajan2017ig} integrates gradients from a baseline image to the input, LRP~\cite{bach2015lrp} propagates the output score backwards while conserving relevance, and SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap} assigns Shapley values, usually computed over superpixels for images. All four are \emph{post-hoc}: they explain an already-trained model, whereas self-explainable models build interpretability into the architecture~\cite{hou2024sxai}, which \citet{rudin2019stop} argues high-stakes domains should prefer.
 
\subsection{Faithfulness, Plausibility and Simulatability}
\label{sec:properties}
An explanation is \emph{faithful} if it reflects the reasoning the model used, and \emph{plausible} if it convinces a human~\cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}; in medical imaging, plausibility is usually judged by overlap with lesion masks. A map can outline a tumour while the model relies on something else, so faithfulness must be tested against the model. The \emph{randomisation test}~\cite{adebayo2018sanity} randomises the model's weights: a map that barely changes cannot reflect what the model learned. The \emph{deletion test}~\cite{petsiuk2018rise} removes the most salient pixels first: for a faithful map, the class score falls faster than when random pixels are removed. \emph{Simulatability}, whether a person can predict the model's output from the explanation, is a third property, which CX-ToM~\cite{akula2022cxtom} measures as users' ability to predict the model. \citet{hou2024sxai} list faithfulness and plausibility as separate requirements for medical explanations.
 
\subsection{Known Failures}
Under the randomisation test, Guided Backpropagation and Guided Grad-CAM maps barely changed, while Grad-CAM passed~\cite{adebayo2018sanity}. On chest radiographs, all eight methods tested by \citet{arun2021trustworthiness} failed at least one trustworthiness criterion, and all seven tested by \citet{saporta2022benchmarking} localised pathologies worse than radiologists. \citet{degrave2021shortcuts} showed why this matters clinically: COVID-19 classifiers relied on shortcuts such as text markers rather than lung pathology.
\section{Audit Methodology}
\label{sec:audit-framework}
We draw on \citet{hou2024sxai}'s distinction between faithfulness and plausibility. For faithfulness, we check model-weight randomisation (R)~\cite{adebayo2018sanity} and attribution-guided perturbation (F)~\cite{petsiuk2018rise}. For plausibility, we check quantitative agreement with lesion masks (L). We also check whether users predict model outputs (S)~\cite{akula2022cxtom}, and separately record user ratings or diagnosis decisions (U).

We apply these criteria to five papers selected for contrasting evidence. Table~\ref{tab:audit} records reporting status for each criterion. Reported results do not automatically mean a test passes; missing evidence does not mean it fails. This sample does not estimate reporting rates across the field.

\citet{panboonyuen2026seeing} defines localisation and perturbation measures (Section II-C), but reports classification scores and example heatmaps without numerical explanation outcomes. Its consistency across seeds is not a model-weight randomisation test. \citet{mcgonagle2026combined} shows maps, masks and SHAP sign percentages (Sections 4.1 and 4.2), without outcomes for our five criteria. These examples still provide qualitative evidence.

\citet{wen2025transparent} reports fidelity scores (Section VI-C), but does not make clear how the map selects the perturbation or specify the attack and its strength. We cannot tell whether this tests explanation faithfulness or classifier robustness. \citet{wagner2026usercentric} reports ratings and diagnosis decisions from 33 medical participants (Sections 4 and 5). These concern user responses; participants do not predict model outputs.

\citet{ma2026ranking} reports attribution-guided perturbation and lesion-agreement results (Section 2.4 and Table 1). Vanilla Gradient's mean rank is 4.7 for plausibility and 8.4 for faithfulness, showing that the rankings differ. None of the five papers reports model-weight randomisation. Our experiment in Section~\ref{sec:experiment} combines this check with localisation and deletion on the same maps.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiment}
We apply the tests the audit found missing to a typical medical imaging setup: one plausibility test and two faithfulness tests on the same maps. Code and results are in the project repository.

\textbf{Data and model.} The LGG MRI Segmentation dataset~\cite{buda2019lgg} contains 3,929 axial brain MRI slices from 110 patients with lower-grade glioma, each with a manual tumour mask. The pre-contrast, FLAIR and post-contrast sequences form the three channels of a $256\times256$ image, labelled \emph{tumour} if its mask contains any tumour pixel. Patients are split 77/16/17 into training, validation and test sets. An ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18~\cite{he2016resnet} fine-tuned on the training patients reaches accuracy 0.942 and AUC 0.976 on the 674 test slices.

\textbf{Explanations.} Gradient~\cite{simonyan2014saliency}, Guided Backpropagation~\cite{springenberg2015guided}, Integrated Gradients (IG; 32 steps, black baseline)~\cite{sundararajan2017ig} and Grad-CAM on the last convolutional block~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} explain the tumour logit on the 169 test tumour slices the model classifies correctly.

\textbf{Tests.} \emph{Randomisation}~\cite{adebayo2018sanity} re-initialises the network one block at a time from the output layer down, with three seeds, and compares each map with the trained model's using Spearman correlation of absolute saliency over brain pixels. \emph{Localisation} uses the pointing game~\cite{zhang2018pointing} and the share of map mass inside the tumour mask. \emph{Deletion}~\cite{petsiuk2018rise} blacks out the top 1\% of the image, ranked by single pixels or by $16\times16$ patches, and measures the drop in tumour probability. Controls are the fully randomised model, two model-free maps (Sobel edges of FLAIR and raw intensity), and deleting random brain regions or the annotated tumour.

\textbf{Randomisation.} Gradient and Grad-CAM pass: their similarity drops sharply once the output layer is randomised and is near zero for the fully random network (Table~\ref{tab:results}, Fig.~\ref{fig:examples}). Guided Backpropagation fails as \citet{adebayo2018sanity} reported, staying almost unchanged (0.99) when the output layer is randomised, so its map does not depend on the class the model learned. IG fails differently, keeping a correlation of 0.54 with a \emph{completely random} network. Its maps correlate with raw input intensity at 0.66 for the trained model and 0.67 for the random model, so much of each IG map reflects the image rather than the model. Over the whole image rather than brain pixels, this similarity rises to 0.82, because with a black baseline IG is zero on the black background whatever the weights.

\textbf{Localisation.} IG localises the tumour best, with its peak inside the mask on 91\% of slices, while the random-model and model-free controls almost never do. Yet IG is also the method that keeps the most similarity with a fully random network.

\textbf{Deletion.} At pixel level the test cannot tell a saliency method from chance: deleting 1\% of random brain pixels lowers the tumour probability by 0.83, as much as deleting the pixels Gradient, Guided Backpropagation or IG rank highest, and slightly more than deleting the whole tumour. Scattered black pixels push the image out of the distribution the model was trained on and break its prediction whatever selects them~\cite{hooker2019roar}. Grad-CAM, whose coarse maps rank contiguous regions, behaves like the patch version even at pixel level. With patches, random deletion lowers the probability by only 0.04, while every method's top patches lower it by 0.37--0.49, so all four methods highlight regions the model relies on.

\section{Discussion and Recommendations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Plausibility does not imply faithfulness.} The best-localising method failed the randomisation test, and Guided Backpropagation localised reasonably while ignoring the learned output layer. Localisation alone, the evidence most papers report, would have picked IG as the most trustworthy method, consistent with \citet{ma2026ranking}, who found that method rankings change with the evaluation metric.

\textbf{No single test is enough.} Guided Backpropagation and IG passed the patch deletion test while failing randomisation, and only randomisation exposed IG's dependence on input intensity. \citet{ma2026ranking}, who used perturbation metrics alone, likewise ranked IG among the most faithful methods on a different model and task.

\textbf{How a test is run decides its outcome.} Including the image background inflated IG's similarity from 0.54 to 0.82, and pixel-level deletion made every method indistinguishable from random. A reported faithfulness score means little without its similarity measure, region and replacement scheme.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
Provisional Placeholder for this section
\label{sec:conclusion}
Saliency maps in medical imaging are usually supported by agreement with the lesion, yet only one of the five audited papers reports a working faithfulness test. Our experiment shows why this matters: the best-localising method kept the most similarity with a random network, and how each test was run changed its verdict. Plausibility and faithfulness are separate claims that each need evidence on the model being explained, and the checklist in Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion} sets out the minimum a paper should report.

\textbf{Limitations.} The five audited papers were chosen by hand rather than by systematic search and scored by the authors, so it shows a pattern rather than measuring how common it is. The experiment uses one dataset, one architecture and one training run, and the 169 explained slices come from 17 patients. Deletion uses black replacement; blurring, inpainting or retraining~\cite{hooker2019roar} could change the size of the effects. Randomisation resets batch-norm statistics, so randomised networks differ in activation scale as well as weights, and IG uses a single black baseline, which partly explains its dependence on input brightness. Simulatability was defined but not measured, as that requires a user study.
